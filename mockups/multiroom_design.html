Multiroom: the Speakers follow the SonosDesign for Ben to look over before any code. Mags, Monday 9 November 2026. Second draft, 13:00, after Ben's questions.

Everything below rests on this morning's tests. The audio path is bit-perfect with your DSP, the Sonos's own clock can be read to a millisecond, and real music across five sample rates came out in step. The design is what we'd build on that. Nothing here is written yet.

The short version

  • The Sonos leads, the Speakers follow. JRiver plays to the Sonos as it does today. A new part of 24bit7, the follower, plays the same music on the Speakers' DAC, placed on the Sonos's own clock.
  • No virtual DLNA renderer. Sunday's plan had one; Monday's test showed JRiver will hand its processed audio to a plain web request, so the renderer is gone. There's a section on it below.
  • Profiles new for the speaker (Sonos One) and the DAC (Chord Hugo over ASIO), picked automatically from what the devices announce. Anything else starts as Generic. A clock pre-test says whether a speaker's clock can be read at all, and one phone recording measures the rest, including whether it repeats.
  • The three numbers are on screen new: Start, Resume and DAC delay. A profile fills them, a recording refines them, and anyone can type over them or nudge by ear.
  • One new Output on the Play tab, "Multiroom", and two voice commands: "everywhere" to join and "just here" to go back to one room. A pause made at the speaker is treated as "this room only" new.
  • Four stages, each planned and approved separately. Stage one is the follower on its own, from the command line, with your numbers hard-wired.

How it works

Why there's no virtual renderer any more

Sunday's plan was a DLNA renderer of our own: a fake speaker JRiver would see as a zone and play to, with the Multiroom server's DSP applied. We needed it for one reason: to get JRiver's processed audio out of JRiver. The probe proved JRiver accepts such a renderer, sends bit-perfect audio, and applies the server's DSP and format. Then, at 12:11 on Monday, a plainer question: the addresses JRiver gave the probe were ordinary web addresses, so what happens if you just ask for one? The Multiroom server answered with the 24-bit, 96 kHz, DSP-applied file, byte-for-byte identical to the probe's download, and it answered a byte-range request too, which is what seeking needs.

So the renderer was only ever a way of asking, and you can just ask. That drops SSDP, port 1900, SOAP, event subscriptions, and a zone that appears and disappears in JRiver whenever 24bit7 starts and stops. The JRiver set-up loses a step (no zone association). Every finding from the probe carries over: the DSP lives on the server, not the zone; the server's Format setting decides the bit depth; and the follower must never advertise 16-bit, which now simply means it never asks for it.

What happens when

  • You pick Multiroom and build: the playlist goes to the Sonos. 24bit7 stops the Speakers zone if it's playing (it holds the DAC), starts the follower, and the Speakers come in within about half a second of the Sonos's first note. From then on every track, change and jump is seamless.
  • The join at a fresh start is a limit, not an adjustment. The Sonos answers slowly for the first fraction of a second of a track (round trips of 15 to 80 ms instead of 4), and the follower can't place the Speakers until it has heard from it. So the Speakers join a third of a second or so in. Gapless changes don't have this, because the follower already knows where it is.
  • You seek in JRiver: the follower sees the counter jump, re-fetches from the new place and applies the resume rule.
  • Someone pauses at the speaker, or through JRiver: see the table above.
  • The clock drops out (network): the follower keeps time on its own crystal, which costs about a millisecond a minute, and says so once. After a minute without the clock it stops the Speakers and says so.
  • The Sonos stops, or 24bit7 closes: the Speakers stop. The Sonos carries on if JRiver is still playing to it, as now.
  • The Multiroom server isn't there: a console line with the set-up steps. No silent fall-back to the Sonos server (that would be 16-bit, no DSP).
  • Volume: the Sonos's is JRiver's, as now. The follower plays at full scale and the DAC or amp sets the room, which suits the Hugo. A slider only if someone asks.

Profiles only fill in the Adjustments. A recording overwrites them for that set-up, and the line under the fields says which is in force: from the profiles, from your recording of such a date, or typed by hand. When someone with another speaker sends numbers that repeat, the model becomes a profile in the next release. The calibration result ends with a line to copy and paste on the forum or GitHub (model, DAC, numbers, repeatability), so the data points arrive by themselves.

The clock pre-test

Check Clock plays nothing. It reads the speaker's position for ten seconds while something is playing and reports: does it answer, how fast, in milliseconds or whole seconds, and does the counter move one for one with the PC's clock. Green: a millisecond counter, steady. Amber: steady whole seconds, worth trying. Red: nothing usable, and Multiroom says why it won't start. What the pre-test can't see is whether the counter sits the same distance from the sound at each start, because software can't hear. Your Sonos's whole-second clock would have passed the pre-test and failed in the room. That's the recording's job.

Calibration

Three numbers belong to the user's kit: how far the speaker's sound trails its counter (about 93 ms on yours), the DAC's delay in samples (1,660 on the Hugo), and the resume lateness (about 95 ms). One recording finds all three, and also whether the speaker's clock repeats from one start to the next.

The test sound is two short tracks shipped with 24bit7: the click pattern from today's tests (uneven gaps, so it can't line up with itself at the wrong shift), one at 44.1 kHz and one at 192, the second following without a gap. The sequence: the speaker alone; the Speakers alone (the speaker's zone turned down through JRiver's own volume control, which works for any brand); a stop and a fresh start, speaker alone again, for the repeat check; a jump, speaker alone; then the second track, Speakers first, then the speaker. From the recording the solver gets each room's lag against the clock in each passage, the repeatability from the two fresh starts, and from the two rates the DAC's delay in samples. If the user's DSP resamples everything to one rate, both tracks arrive at that rate and the solver keeps one offset; that's right, because that's the only rate the follower will ever see.

The test tracks play through JRiver's Playback/PlayByFilename, checked on the Sonos at 13:15: an outside file started within two seconds. JRiver adds a played file to its library while it's at it, so the calibration tidies up afterwards with File/Delete, which takes the entry out of the library and leaves the file alone.

An honest result for a wobbly clock: "Your speaker's clock moved 60 ms between two starts, so the rooms won't stay within an echo. Multiroom will run, but expect to hear it." Guard tests come free: today's recordings are in the sync folder, and the solver must get 53, 1,660 and 95 back out of them.

Setting up

By hand, in JRiver, once (there's no web function for DLNA server settings):

  1. Tools > Options > Media Network > Add or configure DLNA servers: add a server called Multiroom. Format: PCM 24-bit.
  2. In the Speakers zone's DSP Studio, save the settings as a preset. In the Multiroom server's DSP Studio, load that preset. (JRiver keeps them separate, so a later change to the Speakers' DSP means loading the preset again.)

24bit7 does itself: finds the speaker on the network and reads its model; finds the Multiroom server by name every start (JRiver renumbers the ports); checks the server by fetching a track header and shows what it's serving; reads the follower zone's DAC from JRiver and matches it to the audio device; picks both profiles; stops the follower zone when Multiroom starts so the DAC is free, and hands back on "just here"; runs the pre-test; plays the calibration sound and solves the recording.

Optional, by hand: the Alexa console paste for the voice changes, as with every skill change.

So the user's own list is: two JRiver steps, tick Enable Multiroom, pick the leader and follower zones, one phone recording.

Not in this version

  • Multiroom driven by a non-Sonos speaker is possible but unproven: Generic runs on the whole-second clock and the recording says whether it holds.
  • More than one follower, or a second Sonos. The Sonos app groups Sonos speakers itself.
  • A volume control for the follower.
  • Calibrating with a microphone on the PC instead of a phone. Easy to add later if the PC can hear both rooms, which yours can't.
  • "Play everywhere" starting a build from nothing. It joins what the Sonos is playing.

Settled on 9 November, after the second draft

  1. Phone recordings (m4a): Windows decodes them itself. Every Windows 10 and 11 PC has Media Foundation, and a twenty-line PowerShell script turns an m4a into a WAV with it. Tried on this morning's run one: the fit from the Windows decode matched the ffmpeg decode to 0.2 ms, burst for burst. So no ffmpeg, nothing to bundle or download, and 24bit7 is Windows-only anyway. JRiver was the other candidate, but it won't touch a file outside its library (File/GetFile answers 404). WAV and FLAC recordings need no decoding at all.
  2. Playing the test sound: PlayByFilename works to the Sonos zone, see Calibration above.
  3. Pause through JRiver is treated as a resume and measured in the build. Pause at the speaker is "this room" by default, with the tick Pausing one room pauses both for households that want everything to stop.
  4. ASIO is the recommendation. It's what's proven: at 10 ms it never starved, and ten fresh streams land within a millisecond or two. The override still lists WASAPI exclusive for DACs with no ASIO driver, marked untested, and the manual says to use ASIO where there is one.
